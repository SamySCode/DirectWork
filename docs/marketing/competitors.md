# Competitors, feasibility and revenue review

Prepared 2026-11-13 for DirectWork (UK-first, Leeds, launching January 2027). Written for a risk-averse solo founder with a full-time job. It is meant to be candid, not encouraging.

**How to read the evidence.** Everything marked "(retrieved 2026-11-13)" was found by web search or page fetch on that date. Several primary pages blocked automated fetching (Upwork pricing returned 403; startuploans.co.uk returned 403), so many fee figures come from third-party comparison blogs, and some of those sell competing services. Those are marked **[secondary]**. Anything I could not find at all is marked **[unverified]**. Numbers labelled "judgement" are my estimates, not sourced facts. Nothing here is legal advice.

## 3. Positioning map

Two axes that matter here: **(1) does the platform take a cut of the payment or a fee tied to the match, or not**, and **(2) is it open or curated**.

|                                             | **Open**                                                                                       | **Curated**                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Takes a cut of payments / fee per match** | Upwork, Fiverr, Freelancer.com, PeoplePerHour, Contra (client fee), Braintrust (client fee)    | Toptal, Malt, YunoJuno, Wellfound's paid sourcing                                                                               |
| **No cut of payments**                      | YC Co-Founder Matching, CoFoundersLab, Indeed, LinkedIn, Work In Startups, Bonsai/Indy (tools) | **DirectWork's claimed spot.** Paperound is nearest (council-funded, vetted talent side), but it is task-based and student-only |

**The open space DirectWork can credibly claim:** curated on the owner side (a human reads the listing), no cut of the money, local, and with an accountability record on both sides plus written deal terms. Be aware why it is empty: curation costs founder hours per listing, no-cut models are hard to fund, and a track record is worthless until it has history. The space is open partly because it is unattractive.

Quadrant crowding matters for pricing too. The "no cut, curated" quadrant has no scaled incumbent to benchmark against, which means no proof that anyone will pay in it.

## 4. Feasibility, honestly

### 4a. The cold-start problem

A two-sided local marketplace needs enough owners to give professionals something to apply to, and enough professionals to give owners someone to hire. Both sides leave if the first visit is empty. Practitioner guidance converges on: pick the hard side (here it is arguably both, but supply of good listings is the bottleneck), get it dense in one narrow place, and seed manually ([Sharetribe](https://sharetribe.com/academy/focus-marketplace-one-city-one-vertical); [RaftLabs](https://www.raftlabs.com/blog/two-sided-marketplace-failure-rate), both vendor blogs, retrieved 2026-11-13). Circulating failure-rate statistics (73%, 41%) have no traceable source and I would not use them.

DirectWork's specific cold-start difficulties:

1. **Low-frequency use.** An owner posts a listing a few times a year. A co-founder search happens once. Nobody comes back weekly, so there is no habit to build on and every cohort has to be acquired fresh.
2. **No messaging, no feed.** This is a good product choice for the brand and a poor one for retention. Users have no reason to open the app between listings.
3. **Track-record value is back-loaded.** "Paid on time on 3 of 4" means nothing in the first three months. The headline differentiator is empty when you most need it.
4. **Confirmation depends on goodwill.** Both sides must confirm outcomes, and the payment record depends on professionals reporting. Nobody is paid to do this. Expect many deals to end unconfirmed.
5. **Leeds is small for a niche.** A Leeds-only pool of early-stage owners offering real work to freelancers is a small number. I found no authoritative count of Leeds startups or freelancers (the only figures were a Bruntwood tenant count of 80+ digital firms at Platform in 2021 and a Regus marketing claim of 34,000 digital tech jobs).

### 4b. Real examples of similar ventures that failed or stalled

- **Homejoy (2015).** Closed 31 July 2015 after raising funding. Reporting points to poor retention once promotions ended, customers and cleaners going direct to each other (leakage), heavy losses and worker-classification lawsuits ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homejoy); [SmartCompany](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/startupsmart/homejoy-comes-clean-on-why-it-failed-or-perhaps-not/); reasons conflict between sources). Lesson: local service marketplaces leak, and DirectWork's contact-exchange design invites it. The upside is DirectWork takes no commission, so leakage costs it nothing directly. The cost is that leaked deals are never confirmed, so the track record never fills.
- **Weave (2016).** A professional-introductions service that raised about $2m (Y Combinator, Index Ventures and others), reported hundreds of thousands of connections, and shut down because it was "not financially viable"; premium was $15/month ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/weave-shutting-down-professional-matchmaking-service-says-wasnt-financially-viable), 2016). Lesson: strong usage did not produce revenue for a low-frequency introductions product.
- **FounderDating (2016).** Absorbed into CoFoundersLab via Onevest ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2016/07/28/founderdating-onevest-acquisition)). Lesson: co-founder matching consolidates rather than scales. YC itself could cite only two success cases at launch ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/does-co-founder-matching-work)).
- **Shapr.** A swipe-based professional networking app reportedly shut after acquisition in 2023, from a single hedged source (PYMNTS) **[unverified]**.

I did not find a documented failure of a UK city-level freelance-and-equity marketplace specifically. That is not evidence none exist. It means such ventures die quietly without postmortems.

### 4c. What evidence would show it is working

Targets are my judgement, not benchmarks. They assume a January 2027 start with the founder working roughly 8-12 hours a week.

| Checkpoint                | Healthy signal                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Warning sign                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Day 30 (end Jan 2027)** | 8+ approved listings from owners the founder did not personally know; at least half of listings get 3+ applications; at least 1 conversation reaching a contact exchange; owners return to post a second listing or ask when they can | Most listings come from the founder's friends; fewer than 2 applicants per listing; no contact exchange          |
| **Day 60 (end Feb)**      | 20+ cumulative listings; 3+ contact exchanges; 1-2 outcomes confirmed by both sides; at least 5 owners who say unprompted they would pay for something                                                                                | Applications flat while listings grow (supply problem); owners stop returning                                    |
| **Day 90 (end Mar)**      | 35+ cumulative listings; 8+ confirmed outcomes; first "paid on time" reports; one referral or partner (a coworking space, university or Growth Hub) mentioning DirectWork unprompted; 10 owners saying yes to a defined paid product  | Outcomes remain at 0-2; founder spends all hours on manual outreach and growth is still linear in outreach hours |

### 4d. Realistic ranges, one UK city, near-zero budget (judgement)

|                                   | Day 30 | Day 60  | Day 90  |
| --------------------------------- | ------ | ------- | ------- |
| Professional sign-ups             | 40-150 | 100-300 | 150-500 |
| Owner sign-ups                    | 10-30  | 20-50   | 30-80   |
| Approved listings (cumulative)    | 5-15   | 15-35   | 25-70   |
| Applications (cumulative)         | 20-80  | 60-200  | 100-400 |

| Confirmed outcomes                | 0-2    | 1-6     | 3-15    |

A very good result is the upper end; the lower end is the more likely outcome if all acquisition is manual and unpaid. YC's matching tool had 16,000 profiles in about 3.5 months with YC's brand behind it; a solo founder with no brand should expect one to two orders of magnitude fewer. Those ranges assume you run in-person outreach (coworking, Leeds Digital events, university enterprise teams) at least weekly.

### 4e. Biggest reasons it could fail, and what must be true

1. **Not enough good listings.** Most owners with a real budget will use LinkedIn, an agency or their network. What must be true: in a tightly defined niche (for example early-stage tech and creative ventures in Leeds), 10-20 owners post per month.
2. **Professionals don't come back.** With no messaging and rare listings, retention is structurally low. What must be true: listings are good enough that new listings alone bring people back, and email digests (cheap) work.
3. **The track record doesn't fill.** Confirmation and payment reporting need effort from both sides with no reward. What must be true: confirmation is one tap, prompted by email at 14 and 30 days, and visibly changes what applicants and owners see.
4. **Review does not scale.** A human reviewing every listing is the product and also a founder-hours cap. Fine at 5 per week; painful at 30.
5. **Legal and moderation exposure from the payment record.** A "late payer" statement published about a named owner invites disputes (defamation and data-protection risk; I did not research this, so treat it as a question to put to a solicitor).
6. **Right-to-work rules.** If a fee is ever judged to be for "making matches", DirectWork may be treated as the employer for right-to-work checks and face civil penalties for illegal working (see section 5).
7. **Founder capacity.** A full-time job and a curated, hand-reviewed product pull against each other. Burnout is the base case, not the edge case.

### 4f. Verdict

**Feasible as a low-cost experiment; not feasible as a near-term income, and not demonstrated as a scalable business.** The running cost of about £34/month makes it cheap to try, and the downside is bounded: your time. The odds that Leeds alone becomes self-sustaining are, in my judgement, low (well under one in five). The odds that you can produce a small, honest dataset by April 2027 on whether owners post and professionals apply are good.

Conditions for continuing past 90 days:

- At least 8 confirmed outcomes and 35 cumulative listings, from owners you did not know personally.
- At least 10 owners say yes to a specific paid product, and at least 3 pay anything (including a deposit) when asked.
- Weekly founder time stays under about 12 hours.
- A written answer on the right-to-work question exists before any charge goes live.

If at day 90 the dataset is under half of those thresholds, stop adding features, keep the site up at £34/month, and treat it as a finished experiment.

## 5. Revenue model review

### 5a. The legal frame (what I could and could not confirm)

**Right-to-work "online matching service".** The Home Office Employer's guide to right to work checks (1 October 2026) defines an online matching service as a business that **(a)** keeps a register of service providers to match them with potential clients or customers, **(b)** provides an online service by which potential clients can submit enquiries to be matched, and **(c)** charges a fee or commission in return for making such matches. The service provider is "a person providing, or seeking to provide, work or services for remuneration". Where it applies, the online matching service is treated as the employer for the check, and may face a civil penalty where an individual works illegally. Example 4 (cleaner matched to homeowner) is the model case; civil penalties can only apply to engagements starting on or after 1 October 2026 ([Home Office guide PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ad9dfe29ea4857e15ab3294/Employer_s_guide_to_right_to_work_checks.pdf), read in full for the definition and examples on 2026-11-13; the definition is also summarised by [Kingsley Napley](https://www.kingsleynapley.co.uk/services/expertise/immigration/faqs-the-expansion-of-right-to-work-checks-to-non-employees/)).

The guide also says it does not apply to individuals running an independent business, in their own name or through their own company, who contract directly with clients, including typical business-to-business supply of services. But individuals who get work through a platform without operating an independent business may still be in scope.

What this does and does not say for DirectWork:

- **Three limbs, all required.** A pure job-board style model (owner posts a listing, professional applies) arguably does not meet (b), where clients submit enquiries to be matched, and a free service does not meet (c).
- **Nothing in the guide says whether a flat listing fee, subscription or featured placement counts as a fee "in return for making such matches".** I found no guidance or case law either way. A flat fee for visibility is not obviously a fee for a match. It is also not obviously outside the wording, and DirectWork's own "confirmed outcome" record is evidence that matches were made.
- **A fee tied to a successful match (commission, success fee) is the case the definition describes.**
- **Equity and co-founder deals** may not be "work or services for remuneration" in the usual sense; not addressed in the guide.
- I did not find a published list of exclusions for advertising-only job boards. One search summary inferred that advertising-only boards fall outside; that is inference, not text.

**Action:** put the question in writing to a solicitor (employment or immigration, fixed fee) with DirectWork's exact flows, or to the Home Office employer enquiry line, and keep the reply. Until then, treat fees as unresolved. I can't estimate the cost of a solicitor's opinion from sources; get a quote.

**Employment-agency rules.** The Conduct of Employment Agencies and Employment Businesses Regulations 2003 ban charging work-seekers a fee for finding or trying to find work (Reg 10), with narrow exceptions for some entertainment and modelling work. Charging hirers is not regulated by the Act, per the government guidance ([Conduct Regulations guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/conduct-regulations-2003-guidance-for-employment-agencies-and-employment-businesses), updated 22 June 2026). An "employment agency" introduces work-seekers to be employed under contract with a hirer ([Fair Work Agency note](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fair-work-agency-note-acting-as-an-intermediary-between-persons-seeking-work-and-a-hirer/acting-as-an-intermediary-between-persons-seeking-work-and-a-hirer)). That page does not say whether job boards or introductions for self-employed or equity roles are in scope, and it recommends contacting a Fair Work Agency inspector when unsure. So: **never charge professionals anything** (already DirectWork's rule), and ask the same solicitor whether DirectWork counts as an "employment agency" at all.

### 5d. Recommendation

**Primary: stay free, and test a standalone Deal Sheet toolkit for owners** (option a). It is a fee for a document service, not for a match, so it has the lowest legal exposure of the owner-paid options. It uses what you have already built, and it targets the differentiator rather than reach. Make it available to anyone who creates a Deal Sheet and keep it separate from listing, review and ranking. Never tie it to a match.

**Fallback: sponsorship and partnerships** (option b), starting with coworking spaces, a university enterprise team and the Growth Hub. Pursue it from day one anyway, because partners are also your best source of supply, which solves the problem you actually have (cold start) rather than the problem you do not have yet (revenue).

**Keep the current £9 listing / subscription plan switched off.** Not rejected, but it relies on an unresolved legal reading and charges for the thing that is scarce.

**Do not pursue** commission or success fees, and defer university licences until there are two or three real communities asking for it.

### 5e. What to test first and how, for almost no money

1. **Owner interviews (cost £0).** 10 calls with real owners in the first 60 days. Ask: what did you last pay to hire a freelancer or find a partner; what went wrong on payment or scope; would you use a Deal Sheet pack, and what would you pay.
2. **A manual Deal Sheet pack (cost £0 to about £30).** Deliver the first 5 as hand-made PDFs; track whether owners ask for a second.
3. **A pre-order smoke test.** A one-line Stripe Payment Link for a "Deal Sheet pack, 12 months" at £9-£19, offered only to owners who said yes in the interview. Frame it as a standalone document product, state refunds, and do not mention listings or matching in it. Even this, strictly, takes a payment; get the solicitor's reply before collecting money, or collect only a written "I would pay £X" commitment until then.
4. **Sponsor outreach.** Ask three local hosts for a £50-£150/month or in-kind sponsorship in exchange for a named "Founding partner" line and a room for monthly meet-ups. The test is whether any say yes.

### 5f. Trigger for switching any charge on

Switch on a charge only when **all** of the following are true:

1. A **written** legal answer exists (solicitor opinion or a recorded reply from the Home Office/Fair Work Agency) that the specific charge is outside the "fee or commission for making matches" limb and outside the agency fee ban.
2. At least **15 approved listings per month** for two consecutive months and at least **8 confirmed outcomes**, so that you are charging a community that already works.
3. At least **10 owners** have said in writing they would pay the specific price for the specific product, and at least **3** have paid a deposit or pre-order.
4. Workload check: charging adds under 2 hours a week of support.
5. The mobile app does not offer purchase (per `docs/MONETIZATION.md`, purchase UI is web-only because of App Store and Google Play rules).

## 6. Implications

### 6a. The three messages that differentiate most

1. **"A person reads every listing before it goes live, and we take no cut of what you earn or pay."** No one in the set is both curated and cut-free (positioning map above). Keep this claim strictly true; it stops being true if commission is ever added, and it must be reconsidered if any fee is added.
2. **"Know who pays on time before you apply."** The only comparable signal is Upwork's, and it exists because Upwork holds the money. For a local micro-owner there is nothing like it. Launch honestly: the record starts empty, so say what is reported and by whom, and do not claim a track record that does not exist yet. Phrase it as "reported by the professional", not as a verdict on the owner.
3. **"Put the deal in writing before the work starts."** The Deal Sheet is the one feature that also solves a real problem competitors only half-address (Bonsai and Indy help freelancers write contracts; none shows scope, payment terms, IP and equity as a confirmed two-sided record). It is also the product you can potentially sell.

Lower priority: "local, Leeds first" (Paperound already says this, and it only matters once there is density).
